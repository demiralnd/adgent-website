Account memory

The difference between a tool and someone who knows the account.

Everything that makes your account yours lives in someone’s head: the client who will not run video, the market that looks unprofitable and is not, the suggestion you have now rejected four times. None of it is in the ad platforms.

It is why a new hire takes a quarter to become useful, and why every generic AI tool still sounds like a stranger on day ninety.

Adgent picks these up as you say them — and every verdict after that is written by something that already knows them.

Why account memory beats a setup screen

There is no setup screen, because nobody fills one in.

Every tool that asks you to declare your rules up front gets a half-finished form and then quietly ignores it. You do not know your exceptions in the abstract — you know them when something violates one. So that is when Adgent picks them up: from what you say in chat, and from what you reject.

It shows you what it learned and asks before keeping it, and you can override any of it. Memory you cannot see is not memory, it is drift.

How memory turns an audit into a decision

Memory is what turns an audit into a judgment.

An audit applies general rules to your account. A judgment applies your rules to your account — and that gap is the whole reason a good freelancer is worth more than a good checklist. This is the least visible thing on the site and the one that decides whether people are still using the product in month three.

Week one, any tool
  • “Consider consolidating these campaigns” — they are split by client, deliberately
  • “This market is underperforming” — it is the one with the highest margin
  • “ROAS is below target” — that is not the number you judge on
  • Every finding needs the same context typed underneath it, again

Technically correct and wrong about your account. On week eight it is saying the same four things.

Week eight, here
  • The consolidation suggestion is gone — you rejected it in June, and said why.
  • That market is read on margin, because you told it once that it should be.
  • Reports reconcile to store revenue first, with platform ROAS beside it as context.
  • The brief is shorter, because what it already knows is not being re-argued every morning.

Same product, same account. The difference is eight weeks of having been told things.

It stops re-suggesting what you rejected

The fastest way to make someone stop reading a daily brief is to put the same dismissed recommendation in it every morning. Rejecting something twice is information, and it gets treated as information.

It knows which number you judge on

Tell it once that this brand is judged against store revenue rather than platform ROAS, and every report after that reconciles to the store first. Not a per-report setting — a fact about the account.

It carries your limits into execution

"Nothing scales past this without flagging me" is a rule that has to survive to the moment a change is proposed, six weeks later, on a campaign that did not exist when you said it.

Memory is the one thing here that only accrues by starting. A quarter of deferring is a quarter of re-explaining the account every session — the same exceptions retyped, the same rejected suggestion arriving again on Tuesday, the €500/day limit living in someone's head instead of in the thing proposing the change.

Where your rules are stored

Yours, and it follows the brand.

The context belongs to the account, not to one ad platform's view of it. Your rules travel with the brand across every platform you connect, so a decision made about a campaign on one platform is not forgotten when the same question comes up on another.

And it is isolated per company

Each company's data sits in its own database file rather than a shared table with a customer column in it. The separation is structural, so what one account taught Adgent cannot leak into another's verdict.

Before you have to ask

What people ask about the memory.

What does it actually remember about my account?
The things that are not in the ad platforms: the limit you set once, the suggestion you rejected and the reason, the number you judge on, the market that looks unprofitable and is not. It picks these up from what you say in chat and from what you turn down, shows you what it heard, and asks before keeping it. You can override any of it.
Is what it learns from my account shared with other clients?
No. Each company’s data sits in its own database file rather than in a shared table with a customer column in it, so the separation is structural — what one account taught Adgent cannot reach another account’s verdict. Inside your own workspace the context follows the brand, so a rule set against one platform still holds on the others you connect.
How long before the memory is worth anything?
The first rule holds the moment you state it: say nothing scales past €500 a day without flagging you, and the next proposal that crosses it is flagged instead of applied. The compounding part takes weeks. By week eight the rejected suggestion is gone and the brief is shorter, because what it already knows is not re-argued every morning. The daily verdict →
What happens when someone new starts working on the account?
They inherit the context instead of rebuilding it. The rules belong to the account rather than to whoever set them, so the €500 a day limit, the consolidation you rejected in June and the number you judge on are all still being applied — and all still visible, and open to being overridden. That is the quarter a new hire usually spends becoming useful.
