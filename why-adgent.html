Why Adgent

Analyze or act. Everything else makes you choose.

The tools that understand your account cannot touch it. The ones that touch it do not understand it. You end up owning both and doing the joining yourself, every morning, in your head. Adgent was built as one thing — and the reason that is hard is the reason it is worth buying.

The four options on the table

Every one of them is a reasonable purchase.

This is not a page about bad products. Each category below does its job well, and most teams own two or three of them already. The question is what happens in the gap between them — because that gap is where the money leaks.

Category

Reporting & BI tools

Good at: every platform in one place, joined to your store, rendered faster than any native dashboard.

Stops at: a chart is a description of the past. Turning it into a decision is still your most expensive person’s morning.

The tell: you have the dashboard, and you still don’t know what to do on Monday.

Category

The ad platform’s own AI

Good at: seeing the auction from the inside, in real time, at a resolution no third party gets.

Stops at: it optimises toward its own pixel and is not neutral about the answer. It cannot see your margin, so it scales what converts cheaply and loses money.

The tell: the dashboard is green and the P&L disagrees.

Category

Rule-based automation

Good at: doing the boring thing at 3am, fast, cheap and completely predictable.

Stops at: a rule fires on a threshold, not a reason — it cannot tell a fatiguing creative from a soft week, and it pauses the wrong thing at speed.

The tell: a folder of rules, and no one willing to touch them.

Category

Another pair of human hands

Good at: judgment, context and accountability. A good senior buyer is still the best thing that can happen to an account.

Stops at: it does not scale and it costs the most. Reading every account every morning is exactly the work seniors stop doing first.

The tell: the account nobody opened this week is the one you worry about.

Notice the pattern: the two categories with judgment can't act at scale, and the two that act at scale have no judgment. That is the gap.

Owning two or three and deciding later is itself a decision, and it has a price. It never arrives as an invoice — it arrives as the spend you would not have approved if someone had read it in time.

What the difference sounds like

The argument above, in one real refusal.

This is the moment the whole page is about. A tool without judgment builds what it is asked for. A tool with judgment does the division first and tells you the answer you did not want — from the account’s own numbers, before the money moves.

Asked to build a campaign
Set up a Meta sales campaign for the winter offer.
Technically I can — the page and pixel are both connected. But over the last thirty days that pixel recorded 4 purchases, against 16,404 initiate-checkouts and 65,042 custom events.

Meta’s own documentation puts the learning floor at 50 events in 7 days. [developers.meta.com] Four a month is about 8% of it. The campaign would spend and learn nothing.
So what would you do?
Optimise to initiate-checkout, which clears the floor comfortably — knowing it carries no purchase value, so judge it on bookings rather than on the number Meta reports back. Or fix the purchase event first and launch in a fortnight. Either way I would not build it as asked.
Why it said nothe account’s own pixel

Nothing here is a judgement call. It is a division anyone could do, and nothing else in the stack does it.

A reporting tool would have shown all three numbers and left the arithmetic to you. The platform’s own AI would have built the campaign. A rule has no threshold for “this will not learn”. That is the gap, and this is what closing it sounds like.

How we're built

Touching the platform is easy. Knowing what to do is hard.

Seven layers sit between a connected ad account and a decision you'd stand behind. Most tools stop at the audit. Everything above it is the hard part.

it stacks
L6 · Operating surface

The morning brief, the queue of what is worth acting on, chat, live dashboards, and every account scored so you know which to open first.

A chat window wired to some data is not a product. The surface your team works in every day is.

L5 · Safe execution

Read-only until you say otherwise. Every write is logged with its before-state, labelled reversible or not before you approve, and undone on your approval.

Pointing a model at a live ad account is the easy part and the dangerous part. A change you can undo is a change you can be wrong about cheaply.

L4 · Judgment

Root cause rather than symptom, compared fairly across platforms, with the decision stated and the money attached to it.

Not "here is what moved" but "here is what to do, and here is what it is worth."

L3 · Memory

Context that survives the session: what was looked at, what was decided, and what you have told it about how this brand runs.

Memory is what turns an audit into a judgment. A generic tool restarts from zero every time.

L2 · Audit

What happened and where — anomalies against the account's own history and its peers, by placement, device and geography. Then the same read on the creative: its DNA, scene by scene, and where viewers drop off.

Everyone can get you the numbers. This layer still only tells you what happened.

L1 · Data correctness

A year of history, synced daily, attribution normalized and conversion events reconciled into one comparable shape across platforms.

Skip this and you get answers delivered with total confidence and no basis — the most expensive kind of wrong.

L0 · Connectivity

Reads your accounts directly from the ad platforms, with analytics and store data alongside.

This only answers "can it reach the account" — and it is getting cheaper for everyone, which helps us, because it is not what we sell.

  5. where most tools stop

The audit is the ceiling of the category. Memory, judgment and safe execution are the three layers above it — and they're where the work of the last year went.

Why the order matters

You cannot buy layer four without the three under it.

This is not a feature list where you pick the rows you like. Each layer is load-bearing for the one above it, which is why a tool that starts at the top produces confident answers with nothing underneath them.

Judgment needs memory

A verdict is a position, and a position depends on what you already decided. Without memory, every session restarts from zero and the tool re-suggests the thing you rejected last month — which is how people stop reading the output.

Memory needs correct data

Remembering a wrong number is worse than not remembering. Attribution windows differ per platform, conversion events are named inconsistently, and a naive join across Meta and Google produces a comparison that looks clean and is not.

Execution needs all of it

Writing to a live ad account is easy to build and expensive to get wrong. A write is only safe when the system knows what the number means, what you already decided, and how to put it back.

The uncomfortable version: most of the category is a very good layer two. That is a real product and it sells. It just is not a decision.

The trust layer, in detail

Three locks, and a change has to pass all three.

Layer five is the one people ask about first, so here it is in full. None of these is a setting you have to find and switch on.

01

Read-only until you say otherwise

A connected account starts with no write access at all. Adgent analyses, ranks and proposes — and cannot touch the account until execution is explicitly turned on for it.

02

Nothing applies without approval

Every proposed change is shown with its before-state, what it costs, and whether it can be undone — labelled before you approve, not discovered after. You approve from the same chat where you found it.

03

Reversible, on your approval

Every change Adgent makes is written to a ledger with the state it replaced, and put back from the same chat. Platform-side changes are read and surfaced separately, so drift you did not cause still carries a date and an actor. A change you can undo is a change you can settle rather than argue about.

And underneath all three: physical isolation

Each company's analytical data lives in its own database file, not a shared table with a customer column in it. The separation is structural, so a query cannot reach across it even by mistake.

The honest version

What we would tell you not to buy us for.

A comparison page where the vendor wins every row is not a comparison, it is a brochure. Here is where the other options are the right call.

If you need creative generated

Adgent reads creative, it does not make it. Its analysis is deliberately scoped to what is already running — DNA, story beat by beat, and where viewers leave. If you want ads produced, that is a different tool and we will say so.

If you want it to run unattended

There is no mode where Adgent spends without a human approving. If what you want is hands-off autonomous buying, we are the wrong shape — and that is a design decision, not a missing feature.

If one small account is all you run

Below a certain spend, the daily read is a luxury and the platform's own tools plus an hour a week will get you most of the way. The value here scales with how many accounts go un-read.

The three questions people actually ask

The objections, answered before you have to raise them.

“What stops it doing something stupid with my budget?”

Three things, in order: it starts read-only and cannot write until you turn execution on; nothing applies until you approve it, with the before-state and the cost shown; and every applied change is logged with what it replaced, undoable on your approval. It also refuses — an unreachable target gets pushed back on, with the account's own history as the reason, before any money moves.
